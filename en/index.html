Why this notebook

The idea comes from sociology

Before drawing a conclusion, a researcher frames a question, forms hypotheses, conducts observation — and only then decides, with a review date. Here you are both the researcher and the field.

No advice

The notebook never tells you what to choose. It provides a structure in which your own answer becomes visible.

Pencil, then pen

Write in pencil while you think; in pen once it has become data. Nothing is final until stage six.

A review date

A decision is not a verdict but a hypothesis with a test date — which is exactly what makes it possible to take.

The protocol

Six stages, from a vague unease to a decision that can be tested

  1. 1

    Framing the question

    ≈ 1 session · 20 minutes

    A decision cannot be made while it is phrased as anxiety. The first stage turns a vague unease into one research question — specific, bounded, with a deadline.

  2. 2

    Map of options

    ≈ 2 sessions · 20 minutes each

    There are almost always more real options than “stay or leave”. Before choosing, lay out every path on the map — even the ones that seem unrealistic or embarrassing.

  3. 3

    Versions of the future

    ≈ 2 sessions · 30 minutes each

    Each option from the map written out as a concrete future: the day, the money, the people around. Sketch up to twelve briefly, then expand the ones that will not let go.

  4. 4

    Testing hypotheses

    ≈ 2–4 weeks · small experiments

    A version of the future can be tested without living it in full: a conversation, a side project, a day of observation. Each experiment is small, cheap, and has a criterion written down in advance.

  5. 5

    Field data on yourself

    6 weeks of diary + a few sessions

    Six weeks of diary and a series of short protocols give what desk thinking cannot: how you react in practice, not in theory.

  6. 6

    Decision and review

    1 session + reviews at 3 and 12 months

    You write the decision down in one sentence, sign it, and schedule two reviews. Pivots are planned in advance, while your head is cool.

About two to three months of work — then reviews at three and twelve months. Dotted pages for free notes follow every stage.

Stage 4 · the door

First ask what kind of door you are standing in

Before weighing risks, the notebook asks you to name the type of door. It defines how much testing the decision actually deserves.

Two-way

You can come back. Decide faster: the mistake is reversible and cheaper than long deliberation.

One-way

No way back. Do not rush: experiments and the full risk circuit come first.

Most “one-way” doors turn out to be two-way on inspection — the return simply costs money or pride.
